# Minecraft (browser clone)

A from-scratch Minecraft-style game built with [Three.js](https://threejs.org/) — no external assets, everything (block textures, the player skin, the sky and clouds) is procedurally generated. It runs on GitHub Pages with no build step.

## Status (alpha 0.9)

- **Infinite world with four biomes** — plains, forest, desert and snow-capped mountains, streamed in 32×32 chunks as you walk, in every direction, forever. Biomes are blended weights rather than a hard choice, so a mountain range ramps down into the plains beside it instead of ending in a cliff, and the ground cover changes exactly where the shape does
- **Minecraft's elevations** — the world runs from **y = −64 to y = 190**, with sea level at 62, plains in the mid 60s, and mountains topping out around **150** with bare stone above 95 and snow above 120. Bedrock is ragged, like the real thing
- **Oceans, and water that flows** — the sea fills everything below y = 62, and dig a channel into it and it runs. Minecraft's fluid, rule for rule: a source reaches seven blocks and thins a level with each one; two sources on solid ground make a third, so a channel wider than one block stays full; go over a ledge and it falls, lands at full strength and gets another seven. Water *looks* for the drop — with a hole three blocks away it heads straight for it instead of spreading in a circle, which is what makes digging a channel feel like digging a channel
- **Water drawn as a surface, not as boxes** — the height of the water belongs to the *corners*, and a corner is the average of the four cells that touch it, so two neighbouring cells always agree about where their shared edge is. A spreading stream comes out as one sloping sheet rather than a staircase of floating slabs with the floor showing through the joints. Moving water and calm water are different textures, both scrolling; a flowing surface has its texture rotated to run downhill; a current pushes you along it at Minecraft's 1.4 blocks/s
- **…and shaded by measuring, not guessing.** Water gets its own renderer: the world is drawn off-screen first, with a depth texture, so the surface can look up what is behind each of its pixels *and how far through the water that light travelled*. Everything else follows from that one number — **Beer-Lambert absorption** per channel (red is absorbed eight times faster than blue, which is why a hand's depth over sand is faintly green and six blocks of it is deep blue; there is no colour ramp anywhere, just one exponential); **refraction**, so the sea floor shifts under the ripples; a **shoreline** that finds itself wherever the water gets thin, so foam appears around a block standing in a pond as readily as along a coast, softly and with no line; and **caustics** cast onto the point behind the surface, which is why they can no longer land on dry sand. Then a second off-screen pass from a camera mirrored through the water plane puts **the actual world** in the reflection — trees, cliffs, the sun — scattered by the wave normal. On top: a real swell, four crossing waves summed in *world* space so neighbouring chunks agree along their shared edge without knowing about each other, with normals from the derivative of the same sum so highlights can never slide off the waves. And Fresnel over all of it, which is most of the difference between "blue glass" and "a lake"
- **Underwater is its own grade, not a blue filter.** The same depth buffer gives the world position of every pixel, so being submerged means light absorbed per channel over the real distance it travelled (down from the surface to whatever it hit, then back to your eye — so a deep sea floor is dim as well as blue), caustics moving across everything the sun can still reach, shafts of light where you look toward it, and a vignette. Looking up you get **Snell's window**: the whole sky squeezed into a 97° cone overhead, a mirror of the depths outside it
- **Buckets** — the water was only ever where the sea put it. Now you can dip a bucket in it and pour it out anywhere: a pond on a clifftop, a moat, a waterfall down the side of whatever you built. It fills from a *source* and never from flowing water, which is Minecraft's rule and the right one — scooping a stream would take a level that upstream replaces on the next tick, so the bucket would be a tap that never runs dry. What you pour out is a source like any other, so it spreads its seven blocks, finds holes, and makes a new source wherever two of them sit on solid ground. Buckets aim with their own raycast: block targeting goes *through* water on purpose, so that you can build in the shallows, and a bucket that could not see the sea would have nothing to fill from. The two buckets aim differently, and it took using one to notice — a full one stops at any water, because the shallow flowing edge of a pond is somewhere to pour, while an empty one is looking for the only thing it can take and so looks *through* flow to the source behind it. A stream running over the sea, a waterfall in front of a pool, the thin spreading edge of the pond you are topping up: none of them stand between the bucket and the water it is pointed at any more
- **Splashes** — jumping into water throws a crown of droplets and a collar of foam, sized by how hard you hit it; bubbles come off you while you are under; a swimmer leaves a wake; and anything falling throws spray where it lands. One pooled buffer, one draw call, nothing allocated while the game is running. They are ordinary scene geometry, which is what makes them composite correctly with the water for free: a droplet above the surface writes depth so the water stays behind it, and a bubble below it gets absorbed and tinted like everything else down there
- **Water quality is a setting**, because each level is another render of the world: *Fast* is one pass and shades the surface from a procedural sky, *Fancy* adds the depth pass, and *Reflections* adds the mirrored one. The debug screen reports what the water is costing you in milliseconds
- **Swimming** — Minecraft's water constants: 2.0 blocks/s swimming and 2.6 sprinting against 4.317 walking, sinking at 2.0 rather than 78.4, and holding jump lifts you at 1.2 — a seventh of a jump, and enough to reach the surface
- **Getting back out** — swimming up is *not* enough on its own, and the arithmetic says so: it tops out at the surface, gravity gives you 1.2²/2g = 0.022 blocks of coast, and the bank is 0.111 above the waterline. You end up ninety millimetres short, forever. Minecraft has a separate move for this and so does this — swim into the edge and you haul yourself onto it, no jump key needed. It only fires where the hitbox is free 0.6 higher, so a bank works and a cliff does not
- **Swim mode** — sprint in water and you go flat out, exactly as Minecraft does it: the hitbox becomes a 0.6 cube with the eye at 0.4, and you steer with the whole look vector instead of its flattened shadow, so aiming down dives, aiming up surfaces, and level holds your depth. A float pulls you to the waterline when you are near it and lets go a metre and a half down, so a dive stays a dive. The 0.6 hitbox is not cosmetic — it takes you through flooded one-block tunnels you could never walk down, and you stay in the crawl until there is headroom to stand up again. In third person the avatar lies out along the look direction and swims a front crawl
- **...but not in every puddle** — Minecraft asks two different questions and they are not the same question. To *start* a swim your eyes have to be under the surface (`isUnderWater`); to *keep* one, merely being in the water is enough (`isInWater`). That asymmetry is the whole difference between a game where sprinting through ankle-deep water throws you flat on your face in it and one where you run straight through, because a one-block puddle stands 8/9 high and your eye is at 1.62. Wading out to sea still takes you under, and that is the moment the crawl begins — several strides after your boots got wet. And once you are swimming, coming up for air does not stand you back upright
- **Ice** — water freezes where it is cold enough, which here means the mountains: they sit at freezing on Minecraft's temperature scale before you climb them, and the air cools by 0.005 a block above y = 80, so the ice line and the snow line are the same line. Minecraft's rule, condition for condition — a *source* block, cold enough, and at least one of its four neighbours not water — and that last one is the whole reason a frozen pond looks like one: ice can only form where the water meets something that is not, so it starts at the bank and works inwards, each new block of it making the next cell an edge. Pour a bucket out on a peak and it ices over while you stand there. It is written transiently, exactly like flowing water, so a frozen lake costs nothing in the save file and freezes again by itself when you come back; build a roof over one and it thaws. You cannot carry it away — that needs Silk Touch, and there is none here — so breaking ice leaves the water it was made of, or nothing at all if you took the floor out from under it first, and the hole you cut stays open for a quarter of a minute before it closes over
- **...and ice is slippery.** Minecraft's friction is 0.98 against the ground's 0.6, and it compensates in the acceleration by (0.6/friction)³, so the two very nearly cancel: you reach the same 4.3 blocks/s you would have walked at, and the whole difference is that the drag is a fifth of the ground's, so you take five times as long to get there and coast the better part of two blocks after you stop asking (the test measures 1.87 against 0.34). The continuous model here already scales acceleration by (1 − f), which *is* that compensation, so ice is one number
- **Weather** — one clock for the whole world, on Minecraft's own numbers: clear for ten minutes to a little over two hours, rain for ten to twenty, and an intensity that ramps over five seconds rather than switching, so a shower arrives and dies away. What *falls* is the climate again — the same temperature that decides where water freezes decides whether the rain is snow, off one number and no second system, so the valley gets wet while the peaks above it go white. Deserts get nothing, which is Minecraft's per-biome flag rewritten as a threshold on the same scale, and because the biomes here are blended the dry edge fades instead of being a line you can stand on with one boot in the rain. It only falls where the sky can reach: the top solid block in the column answers that, so a roof keeps you dry and the block beside it is still getting wet. The sun goes out first, then the sky greys and the fog closes in — the far chunks fading into an overcast horizon are what make it look like weather rather than a few hundred droplets. And it is the same pooled buffer and the same draw call as every other bit of water in the air: rain is a droplet stretched by how fast it is going past you, and it leaves a splash where it lands
- **Waterfalls that look like falling water.** They used to be sheets of white paper, and every one of the four things wrong with them was a case of still-water rules being applied to water that is not still. A wall of water was given half its churn as foam, flat across the face, which after the gain is four fifths white — so the narrower the fall, the more of it was paint. It was also a mirror: Schlick describes a smooth interface, and at the angle you actually look at a fall from — down a shaft, along a ravine — grazing incidence put it at four fifths sky. Its top edge rode the swell while its foot was pinned to the floor of its cell, so a tall fall pulsed once per block, a venetian blind down its whole length. And the pool at the foot got a third of foam on every flowing cell, which for a sheet a ninth of a block deep is a white apron. Now: the froth is *threads*, taken from the flowing tile that is already scrolling down the face at the speed the water is going; the sheen goes with the churn, so broken water scatters instead of reflecting and still water keeps every bit of its mirror; water inside a column does not bob at all; and the shoreline band is thinness **and** a shore to break against, so the middle of a shallow pool keeps its water. What is left is a translucent green-blue curtain with white running down it
- **Drowning, and the fall the water breaks** — the sea can kill you now, which is the thing that was missing from all the swimming. Minecraft's numbers: twenty hit points drawn as ten hearts, fifteen seconds of air that only runs down while your *head* is under (wading is not drowning), then two hit points a second — ten seconds from a full bar to gone, long enough to swim up from most places and short enough that you have to. A gulp at the surface refills four times faster than the dive emptied it. Falling is the same arithmetic as Minecraft's: ceil(distance − 3) hit points, so three blocks are free, four cost one and twenty-three are fatal — and water simply *zeroes the distance*, which is the whole of "water breaks your fall" and the reason a bucket is a parachute. There is no food in this game to heal you, so health comes back on its own a few seconds after the last thing that hurt you: the food system's job, standing in. Die and the screen says what got you and offers to put you back
- **Caves** — winding tunnels through the whole underground, carved where two 3D noise fields cross. The noise is sampled on a 4-block lattice and interpolated, which is what Minecraft does too, and what makes them smooth rather than speckled. They line up exactly across chunk borders
- **Deepslate below y ≈ 0** — as in Minecraft, stone gives way to deepslate on a boundary jittered per column, so it reads as a transition rather than a drawn line
- **Ore, in bands and in veins** — five of them, and *where* each one is is the whole point. Coal runs from the surface down to y = 0 and is not below it; iron takes the middle; gold and redstone are deep; and diamond is in the last sixteen blocks above bedrock, which is the walk that makes the walk worth it. They are placed as veins rather than as scattered blocks: the underground is diced into 8-block boxes, each box rolls once for a single band it is allowed to have, and what it gets is one blob of that — so a block of ore in a wall is a reason to keep digging in that direction. The rarities come out in Minecraft's order and roughly its proportions — for every diamond there are six gold, nine redstone, twenty-five iron and fifty coal, and all of it together is a fortieth of the rock. There are ten ore tiles rather than five, because below the deepslate line the vein is drawn on deepslate: a stone-backed ore in a near-black wall is a bright grey patch hanging in it — you can see the *block*, which is the one thing a block should never do. Ore is generated by the same two paths as the caves (a column at a time while a chunk is filled, holding one vein for the eight layers it spans; one block at a time when something asks about a single block), and the test that proves those two agree across a chunk border now proves it for the ore as well
- **Forests have trees** — oak logs and leaves, generated deterministically so canopies cross chunk borders intact and regrow identically when a chunk is reloaded
- **Render distance up to 40 chunks (1280 blocks)** in the settings, with fog tuned to the distance so terrain fades out instead of ending at a visible edge. The slider shows what each setting costs
- **One draw call per chunk** — every block face shares a single texture atlas with hand-built mipmaps, so a chunk is one mesh regardless of how many block types it contains. Water is the one exception, drawn afterwards in a translucent pass: a chunk with sea in it pays for a second call, and a third only if some of that water is actually moving. Chunk builds are spread over frames on a 4 ms budget, so streaming never hitches
- **Flat memory** — block data is only kept within 6 chunks of you; the meshes reach much further. Walking with a 24-chunk render distance holds the same voxel data as a 4-chunk one, because regenerating a chunk is cheaper than remembering it
- **Caves cost nothing at a distance** — they never break through to the sky, so the terrain shell above them is watertight and none of that geometry can be seen from outside it. Only chunks within 4 of you get their caves built (16 ms and 497 KB each); everything further is the shell alone, at 5.5 ms and 83 KB. Chunks you or a neighbouring chunk have dug into are always built in full, because a pit you made yourself is a real hole in that shell
- **Dig and build anywhere** — break and place blocks; bedrock is indestructible so you can't fall out of the world
- **...but digging takes time.** It used to be a tap — point at a mountain and it came apart as fast as you could click, which is nearer to deleting than to mining, and it meant nothing in the world cost anything. It is Minecraft's arithmetic now, computed the way Minecraft computes it: per tick, not per block. Your speed is the tool's if it is the right tool and 1 if it is not; that gets **divided by five if your feet are off the ground** and **by five again if your head is under water**, so digging while jumping is a fifth of the work done and digging while swimming is a twenty-fifth — which is why you stand on the floor to mine. Then `damage = speed ÷ hardness ÷ (this will drop ? 30 : 100)` a tick, and the block goes when that reaches one. The 30-against-100 is *can you harvest it*, not *is it the right tool*: a pickaxe swung at dirt is only missing the speed bonus, so dirt is three quarters of a second either way, while a shovel swung at stone takes the long constant because stone is a block that wants a pickaxe. And because it counts whole ticks, an iron pickaxe on stone is 0.4 s and not 0.375 — seven and a half ticks is eight ticks — which is exactly the number the wiki gives
- **The crack is pixel art**, ten stages of it, and it is worth saying why after getting it wrong once. The first version stroked each crack as a canvas path, and a canvas path at fractional coordinates is anti-aliased: what landed on a 16×16 texture magnified forty times by a nearest-neighbour sampler was not a crack, it was a soft grey smudge. Every cell is written by hand now — on or off, lines one pixel wide, jagged because they step rather than slope — and that hard edge is the whole difference between *broken* and *dirty*. It is grown once as a web spreading out of the middle and each stage is a longer prefix of it, so a stage always contains the one before, and each dark cell lights the cell below-right of it here and there: Minecraft's stages are greyscale and lean on torchlight to be seen, and this game has no torches, so a crack that was only dark was invisible on the deepslate you dig it in
- **Mining hits back.** The swing is a fast strike and a slow recovery (both first person and on the avatar's arm), the camera takes a small knock and the crack bulges each time a swing lands, and the block sheds **square chips in its own colours** — a few per crack stage as it spreads, and a proper burst the moment it comes apart. Digging reads as the block coming to pieces, not as a progress bar with a sticker on it
- **Auto jump** — hops single blocks so hilly terrain doesn't mean holding space (toggleable, like Bedrock)
- **Inventory** — 9 hotbar slots ("in hand") plus a 9 × 3 grid, with stacking to 64, pick-up/drop, right-click half-stacks and shift-click transfers. Broken blocks go into it, placed blocks come out of it
- **Crafting** — a 2 × 2 grid in your inventory and a 3 × 3 one the moment you look at a crafting table, with shaped and shapeless recipes matched at every offset, so a pattern works wherever you put it in the grid. Logs to planks, planks to sticks, and out of those a table, a furnace, wooden and stone tools, glass and TNT. Closing the table shrinks the grid back to the one you carry and hands you back anything that was standing in the columns that no longer exist, rather than eating it
- **Smelting** — a furnace is a block with a memory: input, fuel, output, and a burn clock that runs on game time, kept per block and saved with the world, so the sand you left cooking is glass when you come back. Minecraft's numbers — 10 s an item, a log worth 15 s of fuel, planks 7.5, sticks 2.5 — and it will not light at all if the output slot is full, because spending a whole log to heat an empty room is not a mechanic. Break the furnace and everything inside it comes back out
- **Tools wear out** — Minecraft's durability, tier for tier: 59 uses of wood, 131 of stone, 250 of iron. One use per block broken, a bar under the icon once it is worn, and the last use breaks the tool in your hand. Which is what makes the crafting table matter: the starter kit is iron, and iron is not forever
- **Experience** — ore pays on Minecraft's scale (coal 2, iron 3, gold and redstone 5, diamond 10; the deepslate twins pay the same, being the same ore), a hundred points is a level, and the bar sits above the hotbar. There is nothing to spend it on yet — it is the record of how much rock you have been through
- **TNT** — craft it, place it, and click it to light a four-second fuse. The blast is a sphere with a ragged edge, because the chance of a block going falls off with distance instead of stopping at a line, and anything close is certain. Bedrock does not move and water does not burn. Another charge caught in the blast lights rather than vanishing, so a stack of them is a chain. The fuse is transient — it is never saved, and a charge you walk away from is just a block again
- **Mobs — something to swing the swords at.** Four tiers of sword have been in the crafting table since it arrived, with nothing in the world to swing one at but leaves. Now the night brings the two mobs a survival first night is made of. The numbers are the wiki's wherever it has numbers, and where it has an attribute instead, the conversion is spelled out: a zombie's movement attribute is 0.23 and a mob's speed in blocks/s is the attribute *squared* × 43.17 (the same 43.17 that makes the player's 0.1 into 4.317 walking) — so a zombie walks at 2.28 blocks/s and a creeper at 2.70, both slower than you. That is not a compromise, it is the whole shape of Minecraft melee: you can always walk away, and the danger is the corner behind you, not the thing in front. A zombie has 20 hit points, hits for 3 on Normal difficulty at most once a second, and tracks you at forty blocks where most mobs manage sixteen. A creeper swells at three, detonates after a second and a half, and unwinds if you get seven away — and it needs line of sight the whole way, so a corner beats a sword if you can reach one. The hiss plays in full whether you run or not, which is a cruelty Minecraft keeps and so does this
- **Night, in three acts.** Nothing spawns within 24 blocks of you — close enough to hear the first groan, never close enough for the screen to fade up on one — and the surface only spawns after the sun is down: this engine has no light levels, so the sun stands in for them, and while it is up the surface is *safe*, which is what makes it stop being safe. A cave is dark at noon and spawns whenever it has room. At dawn, exposed zombies catch fire at a hit point a second — under the open sky, in daylight, and not in water or rain, so a stormy morning is the one morning they walk away from. The night ends itself; you only have to survive to see it
- **Combat, on the classic scale.** A fist is 1 damage and a wooden sword 4, stone 5, iron 6, diamond 7; axes hit like axes and pickaxes like the clumsy weapons they are. Every hit lands inside a half-second hurt window and the window is a *floor*, not a wall: an identical second hit inside it is free, a bigger one pays only the difference — so a held button swings at the mob's rhythm rather than a timer's, and a mob between two sources of damage can still be finished by either. Hits knock the target back at Minecraft's 0.4 blocks a tick, aimed along your swing, and a sprint hit throws nearly half again harder — which is the whole tactic against a creeper: knock it out of its own swell range and step back. A sword spends one use per mob, other tools two. A kill pays 5 experience and what the mob was carrying — rotten flesh from a zombie, which is honestly junk and stacks like it, and **gunpowder** from a creeper, which is not junk at all: the TNT recipe has wanted five gunpowder and four sand since the crafting table arrived, and had a stick standing in for the powder because there was none in the world. Now there is, and the only way to get any is to fight the thing that explodes
- **The mobs are drawn like everything else here — out of boxes, with no assets.** A zombie is the player's own model proportions (32 skin pixels over the 1.95-block Java hitbox) with green skin, black eyes and both arms aimed dead ahead, because that pose is the silhouette and the silhouette is the warning. A creeper is head 8, body 12 on four 6-pixel legs over 1.7 blocks, mottled green canvas noise for hide, and the face everybody knows drawn in sixteen black pixels. Both cast shadows, walk with their legs and look where they are going; a hit flushes the whole figure red, a creeper's swell grows it and flashes it white faster and faster, and a death tips the body over and settles it into the floor. Zombies groan now and then when near; creepers are silent until they aren't, which the wiki notes with some feeling and this matches on purpose
- **Hunger, and the food that fills it.** A second bar under the health, drawn as ten drumsticks, and the slow economy behind it: walking costs a tenth of a shank a metre and sprinting ten times that, jumping and swinging and taking a hit each cost their own little tariff, and four points of exhaustion empty one shank — saturation first, then the food itself. Health now comes back only while you are well fed (18 shanks and some saturation to burn), one hit point every four seconds, and at zero food the bar turns on you at one hit point every four seconds. The old placeholder — health trickling back a few seconds after any wound — is gone, replaced by the real rule: a full meal is healing, an empty stomach is a countdown. Rotten flesh and raw chicken can disagree with you; the poisoning drains the food bar for half a minute, which is the honest reason to cook them
- **Eating is an action, not a click.** Hold the place button with food in hand and a meter fills over Minecraft's 1.6 seconds; let go early and the meal is interrupted with nothing spent. The numbers are the wiki's — a steak is 8 shanks of nutrition and 12.8 of hidden saturation, a raw porkchop 3 and 1.8, an apple 4 — so cooked food is a *meal* and raw food is *survival*, and the furnace is the kitchen that converts one into the other: four meats smelt into their cooked selves, and a tree's leaves occasionally give up an apple (one in ten, more generous than Minecraft's one in two hundred, because here the tree is the only apple tree there is)
- **The meadow has a population now.** Four passive animals — pig, cow, sheep and chicken — spawn on grass in daylight on their own budget, wander, and flee when struck. They are the food chain's first link: a pig drops porkchops, a cow beef, a sheep mutton, and a chicken both meat and the feathers that go into arrows. They never hunt, they never target you, and they are the reason the night's work can feed you through the next day
- **The night is a full bestiary.** A **skeleton** keeps its distance and shoots — it backs off inside five blocks, closes outside fourteen, circles between, and looses an arrow at your eye every couple of seconds on a straight line with a little spread, so a standing target is hit and a moving one can make it miss; it burns at dawn like every undead, and it drops bones and its own ammunition. A **spider** closes faster than anything else in the night, climbs walls instead of hopping over them — the one thing a wall will not save you from — and leaps at you when it gets close. Both cast shadows, walk and die like the two that came before, and both leave what they were carrying: string and a spider eye from the spider, bone and arrows from the skeleton
- **A bow of your own.** Three string from spiders and three sticks make a bow; a string, a stick and a feather make four arrows. Hold the place button to draw — the longer the hold, the harder the shot, up to 6 damage at full draw — and release to loose, with the arrow flying along the crosshair and spending one use of the bow's 384. The arrow is the same projectile the skeleton's is: the same gravity, the same walls, the same bodies, in the same air. Melee still works against a skeleton, but the honest answer to an archer is a bow
- **Armour, on the classic numbers.** Four slots — helmet, chestplate, leggings, boots — in leather, iron, gold and diamond, worn from the inventory screen and drawn as grey pips above the hearts while it lasts. Each point of defence turns aside four percent of a hit, capped at eighty, so a full iron suit (15 points) takes sixty percent off every blow and a full diamond suit is not invincibility, only the best argument there is. Every worn piece spends a use of durability on the hits it softens and breaks when it runs out, so armour is something you *maintain*, not something you put on once. A golden apple — an apple ringed in eight gold ingots — is the one food that heals as well as feeds
- **Things fall on the floor.** Breaking a block used to teleport it into your inventory, which is what every voxel demo does and is not what Minecraft does, and the difference is bigger than it looks. A full inventory meant the block was *destroyed* — you would dig into a seam of diamond, the status line would say the bag was full, and the diamond was simply gone. Now it lies there until you make room. Blowing up a hundred blocks with TNT dropped nothing at all, because posting a hundred stacks into a 36-slot inventory is a mess; as items on the floor it is not a mess, it is a crater with the rubble in it. And a broken furnace spills its contents at your feet instead of pushing them somewhere. The drops are the same 3D models, bobbing and turning, so you can tell a diamond from a lump of coal across a room
- **...with Minecraft's physics on them.** They pop out of the block they came from with a little scatter, fall at 16 blocks/s², bounce keeping a tenth of the impact so they settle instead of jittering, slide to a stop on the ground, and sink slowly in water. They collide axis-at-a-time against the world the same way you do, in sub-steps of a hundred-and-twentieth of a second however long the frame was, because anything cheaper lets a drop from a great height tunnel through the floor — the collision test asks what is in the cell a drop is about to arrive in, which is exact while a step is shorter than a block and worthless once it is longer, and a frame is not reliably short. A drop that falls through the floor is a block you mined and cannot have. Two drops of the same thing lying near each other fold into one stack, so a wall you mined is one item and not thirty; two *tools* never do, because each carries its own durability and merging them would throw one of those numbers away. They come to you when you get close — moved directly rather than pushed, which sounds like a detail and is not: the first version added velocity, ground friction ate almost all of it, and items resting on the floor crawled toward you at a few centimetres a second. After five minutes they despawn, and they unload with the chunk they are standing in
- **Q throws one, <kbd>Ctrl</kbd>+<kbd>Q</kbd> throws the stack** — which is, it turns out, the only way to empty a slot you no longer want. A thrown item goes where you were looking and cannot be picked up for the better part of a second, or it would come straight back on the same frame and the key would look broken. Throw a half-worn pickaxe away and pick it up again and it is still half-worn
- **Creative mode** — <kbd>C</kbd>, and blocks stop running out, breaking stops taking time, and nothing can hurt you. Double-tap <kbd>Space</kbd> to fly, at Minecraft's 10.9 blocks/s and 21.6 with sprint held. Leaving creative puts you back on the ground. The mode is saved with the world
- **Day and night** — one number, 0 to 1, and everything else is a function of it: the sun climbing out of the east and down into the west, a moon opposite it, stars that come out as the sky darkens, and a dome that grades through sunset on the way. Twenty minutes a day, like Minecraft. The world is lit by the same clock — the key light follows whichever body is up, the fog goes from pale blue to near-black, and the whole thing is keyed to the **sun's elevation alone** rather than to the total light, because the moon rises faster than the sun sets and mixing on the sum made midnight brighter than dusk, which is the one thing a day/night cycle must never do. Moonlight is the floor under that curve instead, which is what moonlight is
- **Sound, synthesised** — no audio files either: block breaks and placements are filtered noise bursts shaped per material (stone a dull low crack, sand a short hiss, leaves a rustle), plus footsteps spaced by distance walked rather than by time, splashes scaled to how hard you hit the water, and the tool moving through air
- **Tools you can see.** Every tool, the bucket and every material in the game is a *model* now, not a picture of one. The hotbar icons were always sprites, and up to now that sprite was what you held: a flat quad, 16 × 16 pixels of pickaxe, hanging in front of the camera. It read well enough head-on and it was a plane — turn a little and it thinned, turn a little further and the pickaxe you were holding was a vertical line one pixel wide, and then nothing at all. So the sprites are extruded instead. Every opaque pixel of the icon becomes a cuboid one texel deep, and the result is genuinely three-dimensional: it has a silhouette from every angle, an edge that catches the light, and a bottom face that is darker than its top. It is exactly how Minecraft builds its own held items, and for the same reason
- **...and the mesher is a mesher.** Sixteen tools at 256 pixels each would be four thousand little cubes, most of whose faces are buried inside their neighbours. None of those are generated: a face is only emitted where the pixel next to it is empty or a different colour, and the ones that remain are greedy-merged in two dimensions, so a flat patch of pickaxe head comes out as one quad however many pixels wide and tall it is. The whole set — four tool shapes across four tiers, two buckets, sticks and six materials — is 7,214 triangles, and the biggest single item is 372. Each face carries its shading baked into its vertex colours (front 1.0, top 1.0, right 0.82, left 0.74, back 0.92, bottom 0.58), which is the trick that makes a flat-coloured object still read as a solid when the sun is behind it
- **The bucket is not extruded, because a bucket is not a sprite.** Extruding its icon gives you a bucket-shaped slab: no opening, no inside, nothing you could put water in. So it is a box model like Minecraft's — a base, four walls one texel thick, a rim built as four bars around an opening rather than as a slab (a box model has no holes, so the hole has to be the gap the boxes leave — write the rim as one box and you have quietly fitted the bucket with a lid, and everything inside it is still built, still correct and never seen again), a handle standing proud of the sides, and, when it is full, a plane of water sitting *down inside* the walls with the dark of the pail visible around it. It is as deep as it is wide, and tipping it over shows you the inside
- **Held right, in both hands.** A tool is gripped low on its haft and canted across the view; a block is held square; a bucket hangs from its handle; an ingot lies flat. That is four poses, chosen per item and used twice over — once for the first-person rig in the corner of the screen and once for the socket in the avatar's hand, at a different scale and a different angle, because a pickaxe that looks right filling the corner of your view is comically large in the fist of a figure you are looking at from three metres away. The first-person rig is three nested frames — a frame that puts the corner of the screen where it belongs *whatever* your FOV is, a pivot that swings, and a holder that poses — so changing the field of view slides the hand without changing how it is held or how far it travels when it swings
- **The swing is asymmetric**, because a real one is: a third of it is the strike, sharp, and the remaining two thirds are the recovery, slower. Tools swing further than blocks and add a roll, so mining with a pickaxe and placing a block with dirt do not look like the same gesture. Underwater the whole item is tinted and dimmed with everything else, and it is drawn on its own layer so that the thing in your hand cannot appear in the water's reflection or bend through its refraction — you would see a second pickaxe floating in the lake
- **Three camera perspectives** — first person, third person from behind, and third person from the front. The avatar holds whatever you have selected, in a hand socket built to take a sword or a tool as easily as a block. The avatar's body turns toward where you're moving while the head follows your look, the camera sits off your shoulder so it isn't parked on the crosshair, pulls in smoothly instead of clipping through terrain, and zooms with <kbd>Shift</kbd>+scroll
- **Mouse look that always works** — pointer lock is used when the browser grants it, but iPadOS Safari refuses it *silently*, so the game verifies the lock actually engaged and otherwise switches to free look: the cursor drives the view and holding it near a screen edge keeps turning. Nothing is ever dead, and the pause menu says which mode you're in
- **Your world is saved** — block edits (as a diff against the generated terrain, so it stays small), inventory, position and view mode go into localStorage automatically. "Reset world" in the pause menu starts over
- **Paused means paused** — every animation, down to the drifting clouds and the idle arm sway, is driven by a clock that stops with the game
- **Settings** — mouse and touch sensitivity, field of view, render distance, water quality, invert-Y, auto jump, and a fullscreen toggle, all persisted
- **Adaptive resolution** — render scale and shadow quality drop automatically when FPS is low and recover when there's headroom (great on iPads/phones)
- **Minecraft-accurate movement** — walk 4.317, sprint 5.612, sneak 1.295 blocks/s, gravity 32 blk/s², jump ≈1.25 blocks, air drag, axis-separated collision that slides along walls, and sprint-cancel on wall bumps. Full blocks are jumped onto, not stepped onto, same as the real game
- **Robust loading** — loading screen, friendly error screen instead of a silent black page, WebGL2 detection
- **Zero build step** — pure ES modules with no bare imports; Three.js is vendored in `vendor/` (MIT, see `vendor/THREE.LICENSE`)

**On a touchscreen, press and hold is the right button.** It is not a convenience — every recipe here depends on it. A plain tap moves the *whole* stack, so tapping planks and then tapping a crafting cell puts all 64 of them in that one cell and leaves the slot they came from empty, with nothing left to fill the second cell: none of the two-plank recipes can be assembled at all. Slots opt out of touch scrolling for it, because a finger held still still drifts a little and the browser would otherwise call the hold a scroll, cancel the pointer, and swallow the gesture.

### iPad

The Magic Keyboard has no function keys, which is why the camera is on `V`, fullscreen on `F` and the debug screen on `G`.

- **With the keyboard/trackpad**: press any key or click once to start — the pointer locks and the trackpad becomes mouse look. `Esc` releases it (the game pauses), and a click resumes.
- **If Safari won't lock the pointer** (it often refuses in a normal tab, without reporting an error): the game notices within 400 ms and switches to free look instead of leaving the trackpad dead. Move the cursor to turn; hold it near a screen edge to keep turning past where the cursor stops. Going fullscreen usually makes the real lock work — the pause menu has a **Fullscreen** button and a **Lock the mouse** retry.
- **With fingers**: tap to start. The left half of the screen is a virtual joystick, the right half looks around, and the on-screen buttons cover jump, sprint, sneak, break, place, view, inventory, debug and pause.
- **The four buttons in the top-right corner appear whenever the pointer can reach them** — on touch, in free look, and anywhere pointer lock was refused. They used to be hidden until a finger touched the screen, which meant that with a mouse they did not exist at all; showing them always would be no better, because a pointer locked to the canvas has no cursor to click them with and they would visibly do nothing. So they are drawn exactly when they work. During locked play the keyboard has all four: `E`, `V`, `G` and `Esc`.
- The movement pad stays finger-only either way — on a keyboard it would be five buttons duplicating `WASD`, `Space` and the mouse.
- **The screens fit the phone you are holding.** A phone in landscape is wide and *short* — 844 x 390 — so width-based breakpoints never fire for it, and the inventory kept its full-size slots and stood 519px tall: the crafting row above the top edge, the hotbar below the bottom one, the planks out of reach. Slot size is one variable now, with a rule for short screens and a rule for narrow ones (nine columns at 40px want 376px, and a 375px phone offers about 313), ordered so a screen caught by both takes the smaller. And both the inventory and the pause menu were centred in a scrolling box, which overflows equally at both ends and puts the top half beyond where scrolling can reach — auto margins centre them while they fit and let them scroll from the top when they do not.

## Tests

```bash
npm test
```

7,388 headless checks across twenty-five suites: Minecraft movement speeds, jump height, wall collision, auto jump, the swimming pose, ice friction, fall damage and drowning; infinite-world chunking (negative coordinates, generation, eviction, edits surviving a regenerate), caves and the deepslate transition; the mesher (face culling, chunk seams, atlas UVs); the inventory model; block targeting; the save/settings/camera systems; pointer lock plus the native iPad bridge; water, in three halves — how far it spreads, whether it finds the hole, what a drop does to it, what it refuses to touch; the geometry it turns into; and where it freezes, which is its own set of rules to get wrong; the weather, which is a clock and a climate and has to be right about both; and mining — how long every block takes with every tool, standing and jumping and swimming, what it leaves behind, and where in the rock the ore is; crafting, recipe by recipe, including the grid resize that used to shift a row into the next column and delete the far one; the furnace's fuel economy and its refusal to light with nowhere to put the result; tool durability down to the last use; the blast maths, the XP curve, the mode flags and the sky's clock; and how a press on a slot is read — mouse on the button, touch on release, and the held press that stands in for a right-click; the held-item geometry, every model in the game built and inspected; dropped items, from the bounce to the merge to the moment they reach your hand; the mobs — their speeds and sight lines, the hurt window and what gets through it, the creeper's fuse and the seven blocks that unwind it, the skeleton's archery, the spider's leap and the wall it goes straight up while a zombie is still stuck at the bottom of it, where spawns refuse to happen and which of them burn at dawn, and the eight box models built and measured against their own hitboxes, with every foot proved to land exactly on the ground it stands on — the player avatar gets the same treatment, including its neck; hunger and food — the four-point tariff, the healing and starving clocks, the poison roll, and the eater that spends nothing on an interrupt and hands the buttons straight back when it does; armour — the sixteen pieces, the four-percent-a-point reduction and its eighty-percent ceiling, the durability every softened blow costs, and the two things a suit is no help against; the arrows — their gravity, their box test, the owner's quarter-second of mercy and the minute before they expire; and the texture atlas, which is bookkeeping rather than painting and fails silently when it is wrong; and the module graph itself, every module imported under stubs so a renamed export breaks the tests instead of the page.

A handful of them are worth calling out because they guard things that would otherwise fail silently:

- **The shell mesh is proved complete.** Distant chunks skip their cave geometry. The test flood-fills the air connected to the sky, then asserts that every single face touching it is present in the shell mesh — so the shortcut can never leave a visible hole.
- **Both cave code paths are cross-checked.** Chunk generation interpolates a precomputed noise lattice; `generatedBlock` interpolates the same lattice one cell at a time. The test asserts they agree block for block, because a drift between them would show up as a seam.
- **The water surface is proved gapless.** Water used to be drawn as a box per cell, and since a spreading stream is a staircase of different levels, it came out as a grid of floating slabs you could see the floor between. The test meshes a real spread and a real coastline, then walks every pair of adjacent water quads and asserts their shared edge is the same edge, to the vertex.
- **And proved gapless once it starts moving.** The swell lifts the surface by up to a twentieth of a block, and a surface quad and the top of the wall beneath it are different quads that happen to share a corner. If they were ever lifted by different amounts a crack would open between them, so the amplitude is a property of the corner rather than of the face — and the test asserts exactly that, by collecting every vertex position in a meshed pool and checking that no position is ever handed two different amplitudes.
- **The reflection is proved anchored.** The shading is GLSL and cannot run in Node, but the arithmetic that decides *where* it samples can, and it is the part that fails silently: a reflection that is out by a fraction slides across the surface as you walk instead of staying put. The test takes a point standing above the water, works out where its reflection should land on the surface, and asserts the water there looks it up exactly where the mirrored camera drew it. It also checks that the mirrored view is a rotation rather than a reflection — a mirror matrix has a negative determinant and would flip the winding of every triangle in the world.

- **Nothing freezes in open water.** The edge rule is the one that makes a frozen pond look frozen, and it is invisible in the end state — run it long enough and every cell is ice either way. So the test freezes a pond one cell a step and keeps the order they went in, then walks it and asserts that each cell was either on the bank or next to something that had already frozen. It also does it once in the game's own world, with the real biome noise: a real mountain 507 blocks from spawn, a bucket poured out on it, and the stream that runs off it left unfrozen because it is still moving.

- **Rain and ice cannot drift apart.** They are the same threshold — below 0.15 water freezes, and below 0.15 rain is snow — so the weather tests assert the boundary from both sides at once: at the freezing point exactly it is still rain, a hair below it is snow, and the flakes fall in exactly the places the ice forms. A second constant for "cold" would have looked fine and been wrong by half a degree somewhere up a mountain.

- **A waterfall is proved to be falling.** The shader reads three channels off a wall of water and gets all three wrong if the mesher lies about them, so the test builds a real fall — a source on a pillar, water off every side — and walks its faces: none of them may carry any wave amplitude, because a face whose top bobs and whose foot is pinned pulses once per block; every one of them must be fully broken at the top of its block and a little over half as broken at its foot; and the exposed edge of water that is *standing* must carry a third of that, because it is an edge and not a cataract.
- **Water really does break the fall.** The same forty-block drop is run twice, onto stone and into a pond, and the assertion is not that the second one hurts less — it is that it costs *nothing*, and that the fall distance is zero rather than the damage being waived. Those are two different implementations and only one of them keeps a waterfall usable as a lift.
- **The break times are the wiki's, to the tick.** Not "about right": the test lists fifteen blocks against the number Minecraft prints for them, because the two constants in the formula are easy to swap and the result still *looks* plausible. It caught exactly that — the 30-against-100 keyed off the wrong question, which made dirt by hand two and a half seconds instead of three quarters of one. It also asserts the two situational penalties separately and together, since a fifth times a fifth is the difference between digging while swimming being slow and being pointless.
- **The player avatar is held to the same ground as the mobs.** It was rebuilt with the mobs — a real neck between the chest and the head, painted face and clothing textures — and the same corner-scan test proves its feet at the origin and its head at the top of the 1.8-block hitbox, plus that the neck is a real part of the figure rather than a claim in a comment.
- **A neck has to reach both ends.** The quadrupeds join head to body with a neck box, and its length is measured from the two things it joins rather than scaled from the head's offset — because scaling works only while the head sits about as far forward as the body is long, and the sheep breaks exactly that: its head is pushed out to clear the fleece, so the scaled neck stopped short and left the head hovering in front of a bridge that reached neither end. The test asserts the chain, not the arithmetic: some part of the model must overlap both the head and the body. It is deliberately only this one chain, because the rest of a model cannot be measured at rest — an arm or a spider's shin is never *drawn* at rest, and a skeleton's bow is placed to land in a hand that has already swung ninety degrees forward.
- **No two faces of a model may z-fight.** A box model does not fail by throwing, it fails by flickering: two boxes that interpenetrate and happen to share a face plane put two surfaces at the same depth pointing the same way, and the seam crawls as the camera moves. It is easy to write by accident, because the natural way to place a detail is to line it up with the thing it sits on — and three of them did exactly that: a creeper's collar at the body's own width, a sheep's fleece starting where its belly does, a neck whose top landed on the back it grew out of. The test scans every pair of boxes in every model in world space and only complains about pairs that genuinely overlap in all three axes, because boxes that merely *touch* — a leg meeting a body — share a plane too and are perfectly fine.
- **Every mob stands on the ground it is standing on.** The collision code parks a mob with its feet at floor level, and the models must agree or the mob sinks — the old cow stood nearly half a block in the dirt. The test scans every box of every one of the eight models in world space and asserts the lowest corner of the whole figure is within two hundredths of a block of the origin, above or below: standing still, a mob is *on* the floor, never in it and never hovering.
- **Every tool is proved dimensional, and proved closed.** A tool's 3D form comes from a per-row depth profile — a sword has a thin blade, a thick crossguard and a slim grip — and the test pins the profile itself: the thickest thing on a sword is exactly its guard, a pickaxe head is chunky over a slim haft, and nothing stands proud of the blade. The part worth having is the step faces. Where the thickness changes, the model has a ledge to close, and it has *two* of them — the extrusion is symmetric about its centre line, so a crossguard standing proud of a blade leaves an identical gap behind the blade as in front of it. The first version closed only the front, which is a hole you can look straight into from behind and never notice from the angle you built it at; it also emitted a second ledge facing the other way, buried inside solid metal. So the test walks each thickness change and asserts the ledges come in mirrored pairs and all face out of the solid, which is the pair of mistakes a depth profile invites.
- **Every pixel map is proved well-formed.** The hand-drawn art is data, so the tests read it: every block face and item sprite must be exactly sixteen rows of sixteen texels, every letter in a map must have a colour in its palette (a missing entry would paint a hole), and the atlas must hold exactly three tiles per definition — the bookkeeping that fails silently as a shifted texture.
- **Every model is proved solid.** A mesher does not fail by throwing; it fails by producing a shape that is wrong from one angle. A quad wound backwards is invisible from outside and visible from within. Two faces in the same place pointing the same way z-fight into a flickering stripe. So the geometry tests are invariants, run over every item in the game: no triangle may disagree with its own normal, no two faces may be coincident, nothing may be flatter than one texel (which is the whole point of the feature — the old sprites were 0.02 thick and vanished edge-on), and nothing may escape the unit cube it is scaled to. They also pin the merging, because that is the part that can silently regress into correctness-but-slower: one pixel is twelve triangles, two neighbouring pixels are still twelve and not twenty-four, and a 4 × 4 patch is *still* twelve, which only holds if the greedy merge runs down as well as across.

- **Items are proved to actually reach you.** The pickup magnet passes an obvious test — "does the drop move toward the player" — while being completely broken, and it did. Pulling with velocity works in mid-air and fails on the ground, where a friction of 6/s cancels most of it every frame: the item crept in at a few centimetres a second, so anything at the edge of the ring never arrived. The test now lets a drop settle on the floor first and asserts it is *collected*, within a second. Landing sounds are pinned the same way, from the other side: a drop bounces several times and each bounce is an impact, so the test asserts exactly one audible landing per fall, and proves the threshold is above the hardest bounce that terminal velocity can produce and below an ordinary two-block drop.

- **A vein cannot straddle a chunk border.** Filling a chunk holds one vein for the eight layers of the box it covers; asking about a single block works it out on its own. Those are two pieces of arithmetic that have to land on the same answer for every block in the world, and when they do not, what you see is a seam — half a vein, cut off square where one chunk ends. So the test walks sixteen columns of rock, sixty blocks deep, block for block through both paths, and separately asserts what the bands are *for*: that diamond is only ever at the bottom, that coal is only ever near the top, that a box holds one kind of ore or none, and that nothing is ever found outside the band it belongs to. It also walks the deepslate line itself, and asserts that every ore below it came out in deepslate and every ore above it did not.

- **A climb is proved to be a climb and not a tall hop.** A spider that "climbs walls" and a zombie that hops one block look like the same line of code with a different constant, and they are not: a hop is an *impulse* — 8.95 blocks/s against 32 blocks/s² of gravity buys 1.25 blocks, exactly the one step it is for — while a climb is a *rate*. Hand a 3.2 blocks/s climb to gravity the same way and it buys 0.16 blocks, which is less than a doorstep, so the climber ends up worse at walls than the zombie beside it and nothing about the code looks wrong. So the test does not check the constant; it builds walls and asserts the outcome. A spider gets over the one-block step. A spider gets over the three-block wall. A zombie, on the same wall, does not. And three seconds into a ten-block wall the spider is five blocks up it with full health and a fall distance of zero, because going up under your own power is not falling.
- **Armour is proved to be no help against drowning or starving.** It is Minecraft's rule, and here it is also the difference between armour existing and armour evaporating. Drowning is not a blow, it is a *rate*: it calls `hurt` once per physics step, a hundred and twenty times a second, for a sixtieth of a hit point each time — and a piece of armour spends at least one use of durability on every hit it softens. Run the one through the other and a full suit of iron is scrap in under a second and a half of swimming, with nothing in the code looking like a bug. So the test drowns two players in identical seas, one in iron and one bare, and asserts they come up with exactly the same health *and* that the suit came up untouched; then it starves a third and asserts the same of hunger.

The tests run on a superflat world (`new World(seed, { flat: 3 })`) so that "walk forward for five seconds" has a deterministic answer, and a fixed climate (`{ temperature: -0.1 }`) where the question is what freezes.

## How the water is put together

Four files, and they only talk to each other through data:

| File | What it owns |
| --- | --- |
| `src/water.js` | Where the water *is*. Minecraft's `FlowingFluid`, rule for rule: `getNewLiquid`, `spread`, `getSpread`, the four-block slope search, infinite sources — and, in the second half, where it freezes: `Biome.shouldFreeze`'s four conditions, and the three ways a cell comes up for consideration |
| `src/water-mesh.js` | What shape it is, and four bytes a vertex the shader cannot work out for itself: depth, shore, wave amplitude, churn. No THREE, no DOM — arrays in, arrays out, so the tests can read the geometry directly |
| `src/water-render.js` | The passes. Render targets, the mirrored camera, the quality levels, and the order the three renders happen in |
| `src/water-shader.js` | The surface. Absorption, refraction, reflection, Fresnel, foam, glitter, Snell's window |
| `src/underwater.js` | The composite, which above water is a copy and below it is the whole underwater grade |
| `src/water-glsl.js` | The GLSL more than one of them needs — the swell and the caustics — in one place, so the surface and the underwater pass can never disagree at the waterline |
| `src/particles.js` | Water in the air: splashes, spray, bubbles, wake, and the rain and snow, in one pooled buffer and one draw call |
| `src/weather.js` | Whether it is raining, for how long, and what falls where — the cycle and the climate, with nothing in it that knows how to draw |
| `src/items.js` | Things you carry that are not blocks. Buckets, and the rules for filling and emptying them — pure, so the rules are unit-tested |
| `src/textures.js` | The two 16×16 tiles, which hold no colour at all any more — only light and shade around a mid grey, because the colour of water depends on how deep it is and which way you are looking, and a tile knows neither |

The passes, in order, and what each one is for:

1. **Refraction.** Everything except the water, into an off-screen target with a depth texture. This is also the image you end up looking at — the composite blits it rather than drawing the world twice.
2. **Reflection.** The world again from a camera mirrored through the water plane, at half resolution, with everything below the surface clipped away. Skipped below the top quality setting, and skipped when you are underwater.
3. **Composite.** Blit, then the water surface on top — the only geometry in the pass, depth-testing itself against the texture from pass 1 — and then the block in your hand, which is a hand and not scenery, so the sea is not allowed to draw over it.

So the world is rasterised once at full resolution and once at half, against once before. The debug screen reports the total in milliseconds; at a 4-chunk distance with the sea filling the screen it measures 1.9 ms on *Fast*, 2.6 on *Fancy* and 3.0 with reflections — a software rasteriser, so treat the ratios rather than the numbers as the finding.

## Known limits

- Free look can't recentre the cursor the way pointer lock does, so turning relies on the screen-edge zone. It is a fallback, not a replacement — use fullscreen and the retry button to get a real lock where the browser allows it, or the [iPad app](ipad-app/README.md), which gets one from UIKit.
- Saves live in this browser's localStorage: a different browser, or clearing site data, means a fresh world. Worlds from before the terrain moved up are lifted 52 blocks on load, so anything built on the old surface lands back on the new one — near enough, though the hills underneath have changed shape.
- **Caves never break through to the surface.** You find them by digging, not by walking into a hole in a hillside. That is not an accident: it is what makes the terrain shell watertight, which is what lets distant chunks skip their cave geometry entirely. Adding entrances means flagging the chunks that have one so they are always built in full — worth doing, but it is a change to the optimisation, not a tweak to the noise.
- **Caves stop at y = 70**, so the inside of a mountain is solid. Carving all the way to 150 would cost a third again per chunk for tunnels sealed inside a peak.
- **The world is 255 layers and cannot simply be made taller.** Mesh vertex positions are packed into single bytes, and a block at the top layer needs a vertex one above it. Going higher means widening every position attribute from 3 bytes a vertex to 6.
- **Six mobs, not the whole bestiary.** Zombies, creepers, skeletons, spiders and the four farm animals are here, but there are no villagers, no Endermen, and no enchantments to sharpen what there is. Mob-vs-mob collision is not modelled either: two mobs can stand in the same block, and the renderer is the thing that would pay for the fix.
- **No crops, no farming.** The animals are the food supply; there is no wheat to grow, so bread is not a thing, and a golden apple would have nothing to gild a normal one with. Hunger is fed by hunting and the furnace, which is the survival loop in its first, honest form.
- **Mobs are not saved.** Like lit fuses and flowing water they are a consequence of the world rather than a fact about it — leave for an hour and the night you left behind is not the night you walk back into. The population cap (16, plus 8 passives) is a renderer's budget, not Minecraft's 70, for the same reason: every mob is a group of lit, shadow-casting boxes, and seventy of them is a thousand draw calls walking towards you.
- **Tools never wear out, and there is only one tier of them.** The pickaxe in your hotbar is *the* pickaxe: no wood-to-diamond ladder, no durability, nothing to repair. Both of those want crafting to sit under them, and crafting is the next thing rather than a number to add here.
- **Ore is something you carry, not something you smelt.** Mine iron and you get a block of iron ore, which stacks and stacks and can be built with, because there is no furnace to put it in. The bands and the veins are the half of ore that is about the world; the half that is about a recipe is not here yet.
- **Healing is all-or-nothing.** Health comes back at one hit point every four seconds while you are well fed and stops dead the moment you are not — there is no middle band, no natural regeneration on an empty stomach, and no regeneration effect to bottle. The bar is Minecraft's; the ways of filling it are one furnace and four animals.
- **Snow does not settle.** It falls, it lands, and that is the end of it. A snow *layer* is a block a fraction of a metre tall and the mesher builds full cubes, so the ground going white means either widening the mesher or lying about the height of the world.
- **No thunder, and no lightning.** Minecraft's storms are a second cycle on top of the rain one, and the interesting half of them is a light that moves — which is a whole system this game does not have, since the sun here never moves either.
- **The weather is not saved.** Reload and you get a fresh short spell of clear, then whatever comes. It is one number and it would be easy to keep; it is left out because a storm you left an hour ago is not a storm you would expect to walk back into.
- **Rain is a barrel around the camera**, about sixteen blocks of it. Minecraft draws the same barrel and then paints a texture across the horizon for the rest of the sky; here the horizon just greys over. It is also thinned on a slow frame, deliberately — the per-frame cap that stops a hitch turning into a cloudburst also means a struggling machine gets a lighter shower.
- **The first ice in a world is yours.** Water only generates below sea level and the cold only starts high up a mountain, so the two never meet on their own: nothing freezes until you carry a bucket up there. A snowy biome down at sea level would fix that, and the climate is already written to take one — `BIOME_TEMPERATURE` in `terrain.js` is a number per biome, and a cold one at 0.0 would ice its own coastline over.
- **Ice is opaque.** Minecraft's is translucent and you can see the water and the fish under it. Here it is an ordinary solid block: the only translucent pass is the water's own, and putting ice through it would mean the surface shader shading something that is not a surface.
- No ores yet, and no lava. Caves are dry: water fills the sea and anything you dig into it, but it does not seep into caves under the sea floor.
- **A planar reflection has one plane.** The mirrored camera is aimed at the water surface nearest you, found by scanning a short way up and down the column you are standing in. Water at a *different* height — a pond up a hill while you stand at the shore — still reflects, but it reflects the procedural sky rather than the world, because it is not the plane the second pass was rendered for.
- **A bucket is the only item.** There is no crafting and no way to get a second one, so the one in your starting hotbar is the one you have. Losing it means resetting the world.
- **The reflection does not contain the water.** Nothing in a mirror can reflect a mirror without another pass, so a waterfall pouring into a lake does not appear in the lake.
- **Refraction reads a depth buffer, so it can only bend light around things it can see.** A block hidden behind another block cannot be refracted into view, and at the very edge of the screen the refracted sample is clamped rather than invented.
- Flowing water is never saved — it is a consequence of the terrain, so it is recomputed rather than stored, which is why a flood costs nothing in the save file. The cost is that a chunk coming back into memory comes back dry for a moment, until replaying its edits pokes the sea into finding the hole again.
- Render distance is not free. Voxel data stays flat, but chunk *geometry* is roughly 0.12 MB per chunk and the chunk count grows with the square of the distance:

  | Distance | Blocks | Chunks | Geometry | Draw calls |
  | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
  | 8 | 256 | 225 | ~39 MB | ~88 |
  | 16 | 512 | 861 | ~91 MB | ~290 |
  | 24 | 768 | 1930 | ~180 MB | ~600 |
  | 40 | 1280 | 5027 | ~437 MB | ~1600 |

  For reference, Minecraft's own maximum render distance is 32 of its 16-block chunks — 512 blocks, which is 16 here. Past ~20 the draw-call count, not the memory, is what costs you frames.

  A shell chunk is ~83 KB and a chunk with its caves built is ~497 KB, but only the ~49 nearest you are ever the second kind — that fixed ~24 MB is the whole cost of caves, at any distance.

  Block data is 255 KB per chunk and stays at ~42 MB however far you can see, because only 6 chunks' worth is kept.
